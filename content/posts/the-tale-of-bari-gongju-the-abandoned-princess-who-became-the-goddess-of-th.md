# The Tale of Bari Gongju: The Abandoned Princess Who Became the Goddess of the Dead

In the quiet, cooling days of late autumn, when the leaves turn brittle and the air carries a sharper chill, Korean culture turns its attention toward the threshold between the living and those who have passed. While many global mythologies feature heroes who descend into the underworld to conquer death or rescue a lover, Korean folklore offers a different narrative. It is the story of Bari Gongju—the Abandoned Princess—who journeys to the edge of existence not for power or glory, but to save the parents who cast her away at birth.

To understand the landscape of Korean shamanism, one must understand Bari. She is the primordial shaman, the bridge between worlds, and the patron deity of the departed. Her story is not merely a fairy tale; it is the foundational epic recited during *gut* (shamanic rituals) to ensure a soul finds its way safely to the afterlife.

## The Story of the Abandoned Princess

The myth begins with a king and queen who, after bearing six daughters, prayed fervently for a son. When the seventh child was born, they were devastated to find she was a girl. The king, blinded by his desire for a male heir, ordered the child to be cast into the river in a wooden chest. The infant was rescued by a mountain deity and raised in secret, eventually earning the name Bari, which stems from the word *beorida*, meaning "to abandon."

Years later, the king and queen fell deathly ill. No medicine in the kingdom could cure them. In a desperate turn of fate, the king was told in a dream that only the seventh daughter—the one he had cast away—could journey to the Western Heaven to retrieve the Water of Life and the medicinal flowers that would restore their health.

The king sent his ministers to find Bari. When they finally discovered her, she was living a simple, pious life. Despite the cruelty of her abandonment, Bari did not harbor resentment. She agreed to undertake the perilous journey, crossing treacherous mountains and rivers, navigating the gates of the underworld, and eventually serving the underworld king to earn the sacred water. She returned to save her parents, but in doing so, she became something more than a princess. She became the *Mugam*, the deity who guides the souls of the dead across the bridge to the next world.

## Filial Piety and the Complexity of Duty

For international observers, the most striking aspect of the Bari myth is her motivation. In many Western mythic traditions, a hero might seek redemption or revenge against those who wronged them. Bari’s journey is defined by *hyo* (filial piety), a core tenet of East Asian social ethics. However, the myth complicates this virtue. 

By saving her parents, Bari does not simply restore the status quo; she transforms the nature of the family. The king and queen, who once prioritized their desire for a son over the life of their own child, are forced to rely on the very daughter they deemed worthless. 

In a social context, this myth reflects the weight of duty in traditional Korean life. It suggests that the bonds of family are not merely biological or social contracts, but spiritual obligations that transcend death. When you see families performing *jesa* (ancestral rites) today, they are participating in a tradition that Bari herself solidified. She is the one who ensures that those who have passed on are not forgotten, mirroring the way living families honor their ancestors to maintain a sense of continuity.

## The Shamanic Role: Bari as the Guide

In the context of Korean shamanism, or *Mu*, Bari is not a distant, passive figure. She is the primary deity invoked by *mudang* (shamans) during funeral rites. When a person dies, the belief is that the journey to the afterlife is long and fraught with confusion. The soul may linger, tethered to the world of the living by unfinished business or sorrow.

The shaman, by reciting the *Bari Gongju Bari-gongju* epic, effectively maps the journey for the deceased. The rhythm of the chanting and the retelling of her struggle serve as a map, guiding the spirit through the gates of the underworld. 

You might encounter the influence of this myth in modern Korean film or literature, where the theme of a "guide to the underworld" appears with startling frequency. Whether it is a character helping ghosts resolve their regrets or a dramatic depiction of the afterlife, the archetype of Bari is almost always present in the background. She is the compassionate witness. She understands the pain of being "abandoned" by life, and therefore, she is the only one qualified to shepherd those who have been "abandoned" by death.

## Gender and the Mythic Subversion

While the myth involves a king and a queen, the agency belongs entirely to the princess. Bari’s journey is a solitary one. She does not marry a prince, nor does she seek to reclaim her throne. Instead, she chooses a path of service. 

Some modern analysts are tempted to frame Bari as a feminist icon, but it is more accurate to view her through the lens of historical shamanic power. In traditional Korean society, where women were often confined by strict social hierarchies, the realm of the shaman provided a unique space for women to hold authority. The *mudang* was a spiritual leader, a counselor, and a bridge-builder. Bari, as the goddess of this domain, represents the quiet, formidable power of the feminine in a patriarchal structure. She is the one who commands the gates of the afterlife, and even the king must bow to her wisdom.

## Recognizing Bari in Daily Life

You don’t need to be a scholar of mythology to see the echoes of Bari in Korean culture. You can recognize her influence in the way Koreans approach the transition of death. 

Consider the *Chuseok* holiday or the *Hansik* period, when families visit gravesites to clear away weeds and offer food. This is an act of "tending to the threshold." The rituals are not just about mourning; they are about maintaining a relationship with the departed. When you see a family meticulously preparing a memorial table, they are engaging in the same spirit of care that Bari demonstrated when she traveled to the ends of the earth to bring life back to her parents. 

Furthermore, the concept of the "abandoned" is deeply embedded in the Korean cultural psyche—the idea that those who are cast aside or forgotten still hold a profound, albeit invisible, power. You might hear the term *han*—a complex emotion of sorrow, resentment, and resilience—often associated with the experience of loss. Bari is the ultimate figure of *han*. She carried the pain of her abandonment and converted it into a source of universal compassion. 

## A Modest Reflection

The story of the Abandoned Princess is a reminder that in the Korean worldview, death is not a final, abrupt wall. It is a journey, and like any journey, it requires a guide. As the nights grow longer and the year moves toward its close, the myth of Bari Gongju serves as a quiet acknowledgment of the cycle of life. She remains there at the edge of the map, a figure of endurance, ensuring that no one has to walk into the darkness alone. Whether in the elaborate rhythms of a shamanic ritual or the simple, respectful act of an ancestor memorial, the princess who was once cast away continues to hold the light for those who have gone before.
