# Essential Korean Phrases for Navigating Public Transit

The rhythmic *ding-dong* of the Seoul subway doors or the sudden, decisive lurch of a blue city bus is the heartbeat of daily life in Korea. For international visitors, the high-tech efficiency of the transit system is a marvel, but it can also feel like an impenetrable fortress of speed and specialized vocabulary. You might have your T-Money card ready, but when the bus driver shouts something at the stop or you need to squeeze through a packed subway car, the textbook phrases you learned back home rarely surface in time.

The good news is that navigating Korea’s transit system isn’t about perfect grammar; it’s about knowing the specific, clipped phrases that keep the city moving. Here is how you can move through the crowd with a bit more confidence.

## The Bus Stop Shuffle: Understanding Drivers

Taking the bus in Korea is a high-stakes sport. Buses are frequent, fast, and driven with a singular focus on keeping to the schedule. If you are standing at a stop, you don’t need to wave your arms frantically; simply standing near the curb as the bus approaches is enough. However, understanding what happens *inside* the bus is where most travelers stumble.

When you are ready to get off, you must press the stop button (*bell*) before your station arrives. If you miss it, the driver is under no obligation to stop. 

**"Naeyo!" (I am getting off!)**

If the bus is crowded and you are stuck in the back, you might not be able to reach the bell in time. In this situation, it is perfectly acceptable—and expected—to call out to the driver or the person nearest to the button.

*   **The Situation:** You are three rows back from the exit, and your stop is next. You can’t reach the bell. 
*   **What you say:** "Naeyo!" (Getting off!)
*   **The cultural nuance:** You don’t need to be overly polite with a long, formal sentence. A loud, clear "Naeyo!" tells the driver, "I am the passenger who needs to exit at this stop." 

Another common phrase you might hear from a driver is, **"Ahn-eu-ro deul-eo-o-se-yo" (Please move inside).** 

Buses in Korea fill up from the front. If you are blocking the stairs near the exit, the driver will likely broadcast this over the speaker. It isn't a scolding; it is a request to clear the path for incoming passengers. Just step back into the aisle or toward the rear, and the tension will dissipate immediately.

## Negotiating the "Transfer" and "Space"

Transfers are a major part of Korean transit, especially with the seamless integration between buses and subways. You have 30 minutes to transfer between modes of transport without paying an extra fare, provided you tap your card on the reader when exiting. 

When navigating a crowded subway car or bus, the goal is to make yourself as small as possible while moving efficiently toward the door. 

**"Jam-shi-man-yo" (Excuse me / Just a moment)**

This is the golden phrase of Korean transit. While technically meaning "just a moment," it is used universally to signal that you need to move past someone. 

*   **The Situation:** You are at the back of a subway car, and your station is the next stop. The person in front of you is staring at their phone, oblivious to the crowd.
*   **What you say:** "Jam-shi-man-yo." 
*   **The cultural nuance:** Don’t wait for them to look at you or apologize. If you say it clearly as you begin to move toward the door, people will instinctively shift to the side. It is a polite, non-confrontational way to claim your space in a shared environment.

If you are the one being moved past, you might hear **"Yeol-da-li-se-yo" (Please open the door)** or simply a grunt of "shim-sil-leh-hap-ni-da" (pardon me), though in the heat of a commute, "Jam-shi-man-yo" remains the undisputed king of transit etiquette.

## The Art of the "Exit"

In Korea, the subway platforms and bus stops are marked with clear, colored lines. When the subway arrives, there is an unwritten rule: let people exit completely before you attempt to board. 

Sometimes, a car is so packed that you physically cannot stand in the middle. You will end up pressed against the doors. 

**"Nae-ril-ge-yo" (I am getting off)**

If you are standing by the doors during rush hour, you might have to step out onto the platform to let others off, even if you aren't at your station yet. If you are standing in the way, simply saying "Nae-ril-ge-yo" as you step out and then immediately stepping back in once the flow of people has cleared is the most efficient way to handle the bottleneck.

If you are confused about which exit to take at a massive station like Myeong-dong or Gangnam, look for the sign that says **"Chul-gu" (Exit).** 

*   **The Situation:** You are looking for a specific street address and need to ensure you are taking the right staircase out of the station.
*   **What you look for:** You will see signs labeled with numbers (e.g., "1-beon chul-gu" or Exit 1).
*   **The cultural nuance:** Korean stations often have multiple exits that lead to different sides of a major intersection. If you find yourself lost, asking a station worker "Jo-gi-yo, [Name of place] chul-gu-ga o-di-ye-yo?" (Excuse me, where is the exit for [Name of place]?) will usually result in them pointing you toward the correct number.

## Etiquette in Crowded Spaces

Korean public transit is remarkably quiet. Even when the train is packed to the point of discomfort, people tend to keep their voices low or remain silent while scrolling through their phones. 

If you find yourself needing to apologize for bumping into someone—which happens frequently in sudden stops—a quick, slight bow of the head accompanied by a quiet **"Joe-song-ham-ni-da" (I’m sorry)** goes a long way. You don’t need to linger on the apology. In the fast-paced environment of Seoul, a quick acknowledgment of the contact is sufficient to maintain harmony.

Another thing to note is the seating priority. You will see seats marked for the elderly, disabled, or pregnant women. Even if the train is completely empty, it is considered standard practice to leave these seats vacant, even for younger people. If you are unsure if a seat is available, look for the signage above the seat. If it is empty, you are usually safe to sit, but if the train starts to fill up, stay observant of your surroundings.

## Making the System Your Own

Navigating public transit in Korea is often about reading the flow of the crowd rather than mastering a massive vocabulary list. By listening for the cadence of the "Jam-shi-man-yo" and knowing when to use "Naeyo" on a bus, you move from being a confused traveler to someone who understands the rhythm of the city. 

The transit system here is built for utility, not for tourism, which means the rules are simple and functional. Don't worry if your pronunciation isn't perfect or if you feel a bit awkward the first time you call out to a bus driver. Everyone else on that bus or train is just trying to get to their next destination as smoothly as you are. Once you get the hang of these few phrases, you’ll find that the "anxiety" of the high-tech, fast-moving system starts to fade, leaving you with more time to focus on where you’re headed rather than how you’re getting there.
